*notes: the article was originally written in spanish, some of the ideas of the article may get a bit lost when translated, mainly the meaning of the word "vorágine" which I don't feel it has a direct translation in english, but basically is the everyday work frenzy and craziness that you can find in a lot of tech startups.*

“Everything used to be better” is something you hear a lot, especially from parents and grandparents. “Music used to be better; today it all sounds the same.” In part, I think they’re right, and in part, I think there’s a certain nostalgia that looks back on past eras through rose-colored glasses, remembering them better than they actually were. Think of a game from 20 years ago that your 10-year-old self could play for 12 hours straight, yet now you’re 30 and can’t stand it for more than 20 minutes. Growing up changes a lot of things. Tastes, friendships, family, thoughts, opinions. The world changes, jobs change. We live in a constant need to adapt, and those who don’t usually perish in one way or another. It’s cruel, yes, but the world doesn’t stop. It doesn’t stop for you, and it doesn’t stop for me. Even so, nostalgia gives us that feeling that we used to be better off, or at least that the world was better; amid all this unstoppable change, some "before" was always better.

And there is something that has changed recently that truly was better before. The frenzy. That feeling that everything needs to be done right now and that everything is important and urgent. It changed. And not for the better.

If you ever worked at a tech startup in the pre-AI era, you probably remember endless meetings defining product, architecture, solutions. That frantic pace was human, imperfect, fun, and it forced you to think, to act, to be flexible. That rush taught you, beat you up, and, once you made it through, made you proud. It wasn't for everyone, and it wasn't forever. We all knew that madness wasn't healthy; yet great things were born out of it.

Fast-forward to today and the frenzy still exists, but no longer driven by a human need to make and create; instead, it comes from the anxiety of not falling behind in a race where nobody even knows where the finish line is anymore. And the question is: what are we trying to achieve? Are there really that many things to ship? How many features can a product possibly have? How big can a platform get? In the rush not to “fall behind,” we simply build and build without purpose, we repeat and copy, and everything turns into a tangled mess of consuming content and features—a game of who gives more, who has more. Everything is about showing how fast I am, how much I deliver; it doesn’t matter if it’s useful, it doesn’t matter if nobody remembers it tomorrow. What matters is today. And it has to be right now.

My quest as a person is to try to push back against this—not against progress, not against growing and adapting, not against new technologies and artificial intelligence. My quest is for us to regain our perspective. To stop and think, to contemplate and understand one another. To re-humanize that frenzy that once drove us to create. To find pleasure again in the details, the pause, the imperfection of the process.

To be more human.
